ABSTRACT 
 
 
 
 
The rising prevalence of Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) contributes to a significant 
slowdown in the discovery and development of novel antimicrobials. This paper presents 
an AI -enhanced framework for the generation and novelty analysis of new molecular 
fingerprints that are potentially active against AMR factors. Traditional Morgan 
Fingerprints were used to train a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) to learn the latent 
representation and generate new fingerprints with diverse structures. The same dataset 
was also used to train a LightGBM -based activity prediction model to predict the 
probability of antimicrobial activity of the generated fingerprints. Cosine and Tanimoto 
similarity, and UMAP dimensionality reduction were performed to analyse and visualize 
novelty and dissimilarity to known compounds. The results display distinction between 
generated fingerprints and known active fingerprints, which validates both novelty and 
biological relevance of the generated fingerprints. Thus, the framework provides a reliable 
approach for novel fingerprint generation and analysis in antimicrobial drug discovery. 
 
Keywords: Antimicrobial Drug Discovery, Variational Autoencoder, LightGBM, 
Molecular Fingerprints, Cosine Similarity, Tanimoto Similarity, UMAP. 1  
1 INTRODUCTION 
 
1.1 Overview 
Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) has emerged as one of the most critical threats to global 
public health, rendering traditional antibiotics increasingly ineffective and resulting in 
rising mortality rates, treatment failures, and healthcare burdens. The process of 
discovering novel antimicrobial compounds has slowed significantly, primarily due to 
limited access to validated molecular data, the high cost of laboratory screening, and the 
declining success rate of identifying structurally unique candidates. Conventional screening 
techniques rely heavily on biological assays and structure -based evaluation, which are 
resource-intensive and often restricted to narrow regions of chemical space. 
 
To address these challenges, this project introduces an Artificial Intelligence –based 
framework designed to enhance the early stages of antimicrobial drug discovery. Instead 
of generating complete molecular structures, which often suffer from invalid syntax, 
instability, or non -drug-like chemistry, this approach focuses on the direct generation of 
molecular fingerprints, a compact and information -rich representation widely used across 
cheminformatics and drug discovery pipelines. 
 
The project utilizes a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) to learn compressed representations 
of molecular fingerprints and generate new, structurally diverse ones. These are 
subsequently evaluated using a Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) activity 
classifier to predict the antimicrobial potential of each generated fingerprint. To ensure 
novelty and meaningful exploration of chemical space, similarity metrics such as cosine 
similarity and Tanimoto similarity are used, followed by UMAP -based dimensionality 
reduction to visualize structural variance and separation between generated and known 
active fingerprints. 
 
Overall, this project aims to accelerate exploratory phases of antimicrobial drug discovery 
by integrating deep generative models, predictive analytics, and novelty assessment 
techniques to produce fingerprint patterns that suggest new and potentially effective 
chemical scaffolds. 
 
1.2 Motivation 
The motivation for pursuing the project stems from the critical gap in the current drug 
discovery pipeline, particularly in the context of AMR. Despite advancements in 
computational chemistry, the availability of AMR-specific molecular data remains limited. 
This restricts model reliability, biological relevance, and the generation of novel  chemical 
scaffolds capable of combating resistant pathogens. 
 
Traditional AI-driven molecular generation approaches often attempt to reconstruct full 
molecular structures from SMILES or graph -based representations. However, these 
methods frequently face challenges such as: 2  
• syntactically invalid molecular strings 
• poor reconstruction quality during decoding 
• lack of chemical realism 
• dependence on large, high-quality labelled datasets 
• redundancy in generated compounds 
 
Fingerprint-level generation offers a data -efficient alternative that preserves chemical 
relevance without requiring complete structural reconstruction. Morgan fingerprints, in 
particular, encode molecular topology effectively and provide a stable input for 
representation learning. 
 
By shifting the focus from molecular reconstruction to fingerprint-level novelty generation, 
this project aims to explore unseen chemical space that may contain active antimicrobial 
candidates, even when biological data is scarce. 
 
1.3 Role of AI and Fingerprints in Drug Discovery 
Artificial Intelligence, particularly deep generative models, has transformed modern drug 
discovery by enabling: 
 
• scalable exploration of chemical space 
• automated hypothesis generation 
• learning-based prioritization of promising compounds 
• reduced dependency on costly wet-lab screening 
 
Fingerprint-based representations provide a fixed -length, informative method to 
mathematically encode molecular structure. Unlike SMILES strings, fingerprints do not 
require exact syntax or graph reconstruction, making them ideal for generative modelling 
in scenarios with sparse data. 
 
In this study: 
 
• The VAE learns compressed latent vectors of molecular fingerprints and generates 
novel fingerprints that capture meaningful chemical patterns. 
• LightGBM predicts the antimicrobial probability for each generated fingerprint. 
• Cosine and Tanimoto similarity metrics quantify novelty relative to known 
actives. 
• UMAP visualization reveals structural separation, confirming exploration of new 
chemical space. 
This integration of AI tools enables the creation of computationally novel fingerprint 
patterns that signify possible antibacterial activity, supporting early research steps towards 
AMR-specific drug discovery. 3

2 LITERATURE REVIEW 
 
2.1 Introduction 
Artificial Intelligence has become a key enabler in modern drug discovery, offering 
scalable alternatives to traditional biochemical screening and molecular design techniques. 
The alarming surge in Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) has intensified the need for 
efficient computational methods capable of producing novel chemical scaffolds with 
potential biological activity. However, the lack of AMR -specific molecular datasets and 
validated biological activity labels poses significant challenges for AI-driven workflows. 
 
This project explores fingerprint -based generative modelling as an alternative to 
conventional molecular generation. Fingerprint representations, such as Morgan (ECFP) 
fingerprints, encode chemical structure in fixed -length binary vectors that can be directly 
learned by machine learning algorithms. Unlike SMILES strings or graph -based models, 
which often suffer from syntactic instability and high reconstruction errors, fingerprint - 
level learning offers improved robustness, interpretability, and data efficiency. 
 
Several studies have demonstrated the promise of deep generative models, particularly 
Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), and 
reinforcement learning frameworks, in exploring chemical space  and producing candidate 
molecules for antimicrobial discovery. However, the majority of these approaches rely on 
complete molecular reconstruction, often requiring large datasets to maintain validity and 
chemical diversity. 
 
This chapter provides a comprehensive review of existing methods, systematically analyses 
gaps in current research, and highlights the relevance of transition from molecular -level 
generation to fingerprint-level modelling for AMR-related chemical discovery. 
 
2.2 Problem Statement 
Antimicrobial resistance significantly reduces the effectiveness of antibiotics, creating an 
urgent global health crisis. The conventional antibiotic discovery process is slow, costly, 
and unable to keep up with the rapidly evolving resistance mechanisms exhibited by 
bacteria. Despite advancements in computational tools, most AI-driven antibiotic discovery 
efforts rely on datasets that are: 
 
• not AMR-specific, 
• limited in size, 
• insufficiently diverse, 
• dominated by inactive compounds, or 
• inadequate for training stable deep generative models. 
 
Existing molecular generative models often aim to reconstruct compounds from SMILES 
or molecular graphs. These approaches face multiple challenges such as syntactic 6  
invalidity, chemical infeasibility, poor reconstruction accuracy, and dependence on large 
datasets that are seldom available for AMR-specific targets. 
 
Furthermore: 
 
• Deep learning models used in molecular generation are commonly trained on large, 
generic chemical libraries such as ZINC, ChEMBL, or MOSES. While these 
datasets contain millions of compounds, they are not specifically curated for 
antimicrobial resistance (AMR)–related activity. As a result, the learned chemical 
patterns are broad and not tailored to bacterial survival pathways, DNA repair 
systems, membrane disruption, or enzyme inhibition mechanisms associated with 
AMR. Consequently, generative models tend to create molecules that may be 
chemically valid but biologically irrelevant for targeting resistant pathogens. This 
mismatch between generic chemical space and AMR -specific requirements 
significantly reduces the biological usefulness of generated compounds and 
highlights the need for domain-focused generative workflows. 
• Most molecular generative studies rely on simple similarity metrics, often one - 
dimensional measures based solely on fingerprint overlap, to approximate 
molecular novelty. However, novelty in antimicrobial drug discovery requires more 
than low similarity scores; it demands deeper evaluation of scaffold differences, 
structural motifs, functional group diversity, and overall chemical topology. 
Traditional novelty metrics may fail to detect subtle structural redundancies or may 
overestimate novelty when only minor bit variations occur in high -dimensional 
fingerprint space. Additionally, many studies do not incorporate multi -metric 
analysis or dimensionality-reduction techniques to visually confirm the distinctness 
of generated molecules. This creates a risk of generating compounds that are 
structurally too close to known scaffolds, limiting the potential for discovering truly 
innovative antimicrobial candidates. 
• AMR datasets are typically characterized by extreme class imbalance, where the 
number of inactive compounds vastly outnumbers active compounds, often by 
several hundred to one. Supervised machine learning models trained on such 
skewed distributions tend to become biased toward the majority class, resulting in 
high overall accuracy but very poor sensitivity to active compounds. This leads to 
low recall for actives, meaning that many potentially useful antimicrobial 
candidates are misclassified as inactive. Moreover, standard evaluation metrics 
such as accuracy become misleading in such scenarios, making alternative 
measures such as ROC-AUC, precision-recall curves, and class-weighted learning 
essential. Without careful handling of class imbalance through techniques like 
weighting, resampling, or scaffold -balanced splitting, the predictive model cannot 
reliably identify novel active compounds, thereby weakening the drug -discovery 
pipeline. 7  
This project seeks to address these gaps by generating fingerprints directly, evaluating 
biological activity using a supervised model, and assessing novelty through similarity 
metrics and dimensionality reduction. 
 
2.3 Research Area 
 
2.3.1 AI in Antimicrobial Drug Discovery 
The application of Artificial Intelligence in antimicrobial drug discovery has grown 
substantially due to its capacity to screen massive chemical libraries, uncover hidden 
structure–activity relationships, and explore regions of chemical space that traditional 
techniques often overlook. AI-driven methods can process millions of compounds, identify 
promising antibacterial candidates, and streamline experimental workflows that would 
otherwise take years. Studies have demonstrated that AI can significantly accelerate 
multiple stages of the antimicrobial innovation pipeline, including, but not limited to, the 
following areas: 
 
• Antimicrobial activity prediction – AI models trained on molecular descriptors, 
fingerprints, and biological assay data can rapidly predict whether a given 
compound exhibits antibacterial potential. Traditional wet -lab screening methods 
are expensive and time -consuming, but machine learning models, especially 
ensemble techniques and deep neural networks, can evaluate tens of thousands of 
compounds within seconds. These prediction models learn intricate correlations 
between structural features and biological activity, enabling researchers to prioritize 
compounds with the highest probability of antimicrobial efficacy. Such 
computational triaging greatly reduces experimental workload and accelerates early 
discovery. 
• Antibiotic repurposing – AI enables the identification of non -antibiotic drugs that 
may possess hidden antimicrobial properties, a strategy known as drug repurposing. 
Repurposing significantly shortens development timelines because existing drugs 
already have established safety and pharmacokinetic profiles. Machine learning 
algorithms can analyse patterns in chemical structure, target affinity, gene 
expression, and phenotypic response to reveal unexpected antibacterial potential in 
compounds originally designed for unrelated diseases. This approach is particularly 
valuable in the AMR era, where the need for fast, cost-efficient solutions is critical. 
• Detection of resistance patterns - AI models can analyse large -scale genomic, 
proteomic, and phenotypic datasets to detect emerging resistance  mechanisms and 
predict bacterial behaviour. For example, machine learning classifiers can identify 
genes associated with resistance, recognize mutations that lead to antibiotic failure, 
and anticipate evolutionary patterns in bacterial populations. This predictive insight 
helps guide the development of new antibiotics that circumvent existing resistance 
strategies. It also supports the personalization of antimicrobial therapies by 
predicting which drugs will be effective against specific bacterial strains. 8  
• Design of novel antibacterial candidates - Deep generative models such as 
Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), 
diffusion models, and reinforcement learning frameworks are increasingly used to 
design new molecular structures with desired antibacterial properties. These models 
can generate compounds that exhibit desirable physicochemical features, improved 
target affinity, or unique structural patterns not present in existing chemical 
libraries. AI-driven molecule generation significantly expands the chemical search 
space, enabling exploration of completely new scaffolds that may serve as future 
antibiotics. 
 
Deep learning methods based on SMILES sequences, graph neural networks (GNNs), and 
molecular descriptor embeddings have shown considerable promise across all these tasks. 
However, these models heavily rely on large, high-quality datasets, resources that are rarely 
available for AMR -specific research due to data scarcity, high experimental costs, and 
privacy restrictions. As a result, traditional generative frameworks often struggle to learn 
meaningful AMR-specific chemical patterns. 
 
To address these limitations, fingerprint -based generative modelling provides an 
advantageous alternative. Fingerprints encode chemical features in a fixed -length binary 
format that is more stable, syntactically error -free, and data -efficient compared to full 
structural representations. These properties make fingerprint -level modelling particularly 
suitable for scenarios with limited AMR datasets, enabling generative models to explore 
novel chemical space while avoiding common reconstruction challenges associated with 
SMILES or graph-based approaches. 
 
2.3.2 Molecular Fingerprints 
Molecular fingerprints convert complex chemical structures into numerical or binary 
vectors, simplifying chemical representation for machine learning tasks. Popular 
fingerprints include: 
 
• Morgan/ECFP fingerprints 
• MACCS keys 
• Topological fingerprints 
• Pharmacophore fingerprints 
 
Fingerprint-based models are widely used in virtual screening, molecular similarity 
analysis, and activity prediction. They avoid dependency on syntactic correctness, making 
them suitable for generative tasks where reconstruction from latent space is not required. 
 
2.3.3 Deep Generative Models 
Deep generative models have emerged as transformative tools in computational drug 
discovery due to their ability to learn complex chemical representations and generate new 
molecular candidates with tuneable properties. Models such as Variational Autoencoders 9  
(VAEs), Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), normalizing flows, and transformer - 
based architectures have demonstrated significant potential in exploring high-dimensional 
chemical space far beyond the boundaries of traditional rule-based methods. 
 
These models learn underlying distributions of chemical structures and can generate new 
compounds by sampling from this learned space. Among them, VAEs are particularly 
popular in molecular research because they combine probabilistic inference with deep 
neural architectures, making them well -suited for continuous latent space exploration. 
VAEs transform discrete chemical information into a compressed latent representation, 
enabling smooth interpolations, structural variations, and controlled molecule design. 
 
VAEs provide several advantages: 
 
• Latent space continuity - VAEs map molecular fingerprints or structural encodings 
into a continuous latent space where small changes in latent vectors correspond to 
gradual modifications in chemical properties. This continuity enables researchers 
to explore intermediate regions between known molecules, discover analogues with 
potentially improved biological activity, and navigate chemical space more 
systematically. 
• Probabilistic sampling - VAEs introduce stochasticity through Gaussian priors in 
the latent layer, allowing the model to produce diverse outputs from similar regions 
of latent space. Probabilistic sampling improves the generation of structurally 
varied molecules and reduces the risk of mode collapse. This capability is crucial 
for expanding chemical diversity and identifying novel scaffolds with antimicrobial 
relevance. 
• Structural diversity - By compressing and reconstructing chemical representations, 
VAEs inherently learn patterns associated with functional groups, ring systems, and 
scaffold variations. This supports the creation of molecules that reflect chemically 
meaningful diversity rather than simple perturbations of known structures. Such 
diversity is essential in antimicrobial drug discovery, where molecular novelty 
directly influences the ability to overcome resistance mechanisms. 
• Dimensionality reduction - VAEs act as effective dimensionality-reduction tools by 
extracting compact, information -rich latent encodings from high -dimensional 
molecular fingerprints. This compressed space facilitates clustering, similarity 
analysis, interpolation, and visualization, key steps in assessing chemical relevance, 
novelty, and distribution. Dimensionality reduction also improves computational 
efficiency during downstream tasks. 
 
Despite these advantages, most traditional VAE frameworks focus on reconstructing 
SMILES strings or molecular graphs, which introduces several challenges: 10  
• SMILES representations are sensitive to grammatical constraints and easily 
produce invalid molecules when even minor decoding errors occur. 
• Graph reconstructions often violate atomic valence, aromaticity rules, or ring 
structures, leading to chemically impossible outputs. 
• Reconstruction loss can dominate training, forcing models to prioritize syntactic 
validity over chemical novelty. 
• Limited AMR datasets further amplify reconstruction difficulties due to insufficient 
training diversity. 
 
These issues collectively degrade model performance and limit the usefulness of generated 
molecules. 
 
To overcome these challenges, fingerprint-based VAEs operate directly on numerical 
representations rather than structural SMILES or graph formats . This eliminates 
syntactic constraints and removes the need for reconstructing complete molecular graphs. 
Fingerprints, especially Morgan (ECFP) fingerprints, encode chemical environments in 
stable binary vectors, allowing VAEs to: 
 
• focus on learning underlying chemical patterns without structural decoding errors, 
• generate diverse and novel fingerprint representations, and 
• operate efficiently even with limited AMR-specific training data. 
 
Thus, fingerprint -based generative modelling offers a more reliable, data -efficient, and 
chemically meaningful approach for exploring antimicrobial-relevant chemical space 
 
2.3.4 Activity Prediction Models 
Predicting the biological activity of chemical compounds is a critical component of 
antimicrobial drug discovery, as it determines whether generated or existing molecules are 
likely to exhibit desirable antibacterial properties. Activity prediction models serve as 
computational filters that prioritize promising candidates before advancing them too costly 
in-vitro or in-vivo experimental testing. Machine learning has been widely adopted in this 
area due to its ability to learn complex non -linear relationships between chemical features 
and biological outcomes. 
 
Although numerous machine learning algorithms have been explored in literature, such as 
Random Forests, Support Vector Machines, Logistic Regression, Neural Networks, and 
Gradient Boosting frameworks, this project specifically employs Light Gradient 
Boosting Machine (LightGBM) due to its exceptional performance on high-dimensional, 
sparse data formats such as molecular fingerprints. 11  
LightGBM is an advanced implementation of gradient boosting decision trees that offers 
several advantages particularly relevant to cheminformatics and antimicrobial screening 
tasks: 
 
• High efficiency on high-dimensional sparse fingerprints - Molecular fingerprints, 
such as the 1024-bit Morgan fingerprints used in this study, are inherently sparse, 
with most bits inactive for any given molecule. LightGBM is highly optimized for 
such data using histogram -based binning and leaf -wise tree growth, enabling the 
model to process tens of thousands of features without the computational overhead 
typically associated with classical machine learning algorithms. This makes it 
well-suited for large-scale antimicrobial screening tasks where high -dimensional 
feature vectors must be evaluated rapidly and accurately. 
• Robust handling of extreme class imbalance - AMR-related datasets suffer from a 
severe imbalance between active and inactive compounds, often exceeding a 300:1 
ratio. Traditional classifiers tend to bias heavily toward the majority class, 
producing misleadingly high accuracy but poor recall for the minority (active) 
class. LightGBM incorporates built-in mechanisms for applying customized class 
weights, allowing the minority positive class to be emphasized during training. In 
this project, a square-root class-balancing approach was applied to mitigate bias, 
ensuring the model identifies active compounds more reliably even under highly 
skewed distributions. 
• Ability to model complex, non -linear structure –activity relationships - 
Antimicrobial activity often depends on subtle molecular features, interactions 
between functional groups, and higher -order structural patterns that are not 
linearly separable. LightGBM’s ensemble of optimized decision trees captures 
these non -linear relationships effectively, outperforming simpler linear models 
and many neural network architectures, especially in situations where dataset size 
is limited, as is common in AMR research. Its ability to isolate and exploit 
informative fingerprint substructures leads to more accurate prediction of 
antimicrobial potential. 
• Fast training and scalability - One of LightGBM’s primary strengths is its 
exceptional training speed even on very large datasets. The RecBCD dataset used 
in this project contains over 360,000 fingerprint vectors, making computational 
efficiency a major concern. LightGBM’s leaf -wise tree growth and optimized 
memory management allow rapid experimentation, hyperparameter tuning, and 
iteration without sacrificing performance. This scalability is crucial for screening 
thousands of generated fingerprints in real time. 
• Interpretability through feature importance - Although gradient boosting models 
are complex, LightGBM still offers transparent mechanisms for examining feature 
importance. Bit-level contribution scores help identify the fingerprint regions most 
associated with antimicrobial activity, providing valuable chemical insights. Such 12  
interpretability is useful for understanding which substructures are favoured by 
the predictive model, even when complete molecular reconstruction is not 
performed. 
 
In summary, while the broader research community has explored various predictive 
algorithms, LightGBM stands out as the most appropriate and effective model for this 
project due to: 
 
• the sparse, high-dimensional nature of fingerprint data, 
• the extreme imbalance of AMR datasets, 
• the need for rapid and scalable prediction, and 
• the requirement to capture complex structural activity relationships. 
 
These advantages collectively justify its exclusive use in the present study for predicting 
antimicrobial activity of both training molecules and generated fingerprints 
 
2.3.5 AMR-Related Dataset Limitations 
 
A major challenge in applying artificial intelligence to antimicrobial drug discovery lies in 
the nature and quality of available datasets. Unlike general -purpose chemical databases 
such as ZINC, ChEMBL, or PubChem, datasets specifically curated for Antimicrobial 
Resistance (AMR) are scarce, fragmented, and often insufficient for training deep learning 
models. These limitations significantly constrain the development, generalization, and 
reliability of AI frameworks designed for AMR-focused research. 
 
AMR-related datasets exhibit several critical limitations: 
 
• Extreme scarcity of active compounds 
• Predominance of inactives 
• Incomplete annotations 
• Restricted molecular diversity 
• Limited dataset size 
 
This results in poor model generalization and limited chemical novelty. 
 
2.4 Challenges 
 
2.4.1 Data Scarcity in AMR 
A fundamental challenge in applying artificial intelligence to antimicrobial drug discovery 
is the severe scarcity of high -quality, well -annotated datasets specific to Antimicrobial 
Resistance (AMR). Unlike general drug -discovery domains that benefit from extensive 
open-source molecular repositories such as ZINC or ChEMBL, AMR research suffers from 
limited publicly available data due to the high cost, complexity, and safety requirements 13  
associated with antimicrobial testing. Experimental generation of AMR -related data 
requires sophisticated laboratory facilities, bacterial culturing equipment, biosafety 
infrastructure, and rigorous validation protocols, making the acquisition of new molecular 
activity labels both expensive and time-consuming. As a result, most AMR datasets remain 
relatively small and contain only a handful of active compounds compared to thousands or 
hundreds of thousands of inactive molecules. This imbalance drastically restricts the ability 
of machine learning models to learn meaningful patterns that correlate molecular structure 
with antimicrobial activity. Furthermore, the actives that are available often lack structural 
diversity, representing only a narrow range of chemical scaffolds predominantly derived 
from known antibacterial classes. This limited diversity prevents generative models from 
learning broad chemical principles and forces them to operate within a constrained 
pharmacophoric landscape, diminishing their potential to generate novel molecules. 
Additionally, the scarcity of standardized, unified AMR datasets across studies makes it 
difficult to benchmark models or reproduce experimental findings. Data collected across 
different laboratories frequently varies in experimental settings, concentration thresholds, 
and bacterial strains, resulting in inconsistent and noisy labels that further degrade model 
reliability. These combined limitations mean that AMR -focused AI solutions must be 
exceptionally data -efficient, robust against imbalance, and capable of extracting 
meaningful latent representations despite limited input diversity. Consequently, 
fingerprint-based generative modelling becomes a suitable strategy, as it leverages high - 
dimensional structural features while operating effectively in low -data environments, 
offering a practical path forward in a field where data scarcity remains a major bottleneck 
2.4.2 Model Reliability and Chemical Validity 
A major limitation in AI-driven molecular discovery arises from the difficulty of ensuring 
both model reliability and chemical validity in the generated outputs. Many generative 
frameworks depend on reconstructing complete molecular structures, typically encoded as 
SMILES strings or molecular graphs, yet these representations are inherently fragile and 
prone to syntactic and structural errors. Even minor decoding mistakes in a SMILES 
sequence can result in molecules with broken valence rules, non-existent atoms, impossible 
ring closures, or chemically unstable configurations. Similarly, graph -based generative 
models often struggle to maintain fundamental chemical constraints such as correct atomic 
valence, aromaticity, chiral centres, and bond orders. These reconstruction challenges 
directly impact the reliability of the underlying model because the training process attempts 
to minimize reconstruction loss, forcing the network to memorize structural patterns instead 
of learning meaningful, generalizable chemical features. As a consequence, the model may 
generate a high proportion of invalid or chemically nonsensical molecules, reducing the 
usefulness of the output and limiting chemical space exploration. 
 
Model reliability is further compromised by biases in training data and insufficient 
representation of key structural motifs, especially in AMR -specific datasets where active 
compounds are scarce and structurally homogeneous. This often leads generative models 
to produce repetitive scaffolds, lack novelty, or drift into unrealistic chemical regions. 
Moreover, the stochastic nature of many generative architectures, such as VAEs and GANs, 14  
can result in unstable training dynamics, mode collapse, or overly smooth latent spaces that 
fail to preserve essential molecular features. In drug discovery, where biological activity 
depends on subtle structural nuances, even small deviations in generated molecules can 
render a compound inactive or biologically irrelevant. Therefore, ensuring chemical 
validity is not merely a matter of avoiding syntactic errors but a requirement for producing 
molecules that make sense from a medicinal chemistry perspective. 
 
Fingerprint-based generative modelling provides a more reliable alternative to address 
these issues. Instead of reconstructing full molecular structures with complex grammar and 
topological constraints, fingerprint representations encode molecules as fixed-length binary 
vectors that abstract meaningful chemical features without requiring explicit 
reconstruction. This approach significantly reduces the likelihood of generating invalid 
compounds, eliminates dependency on syntactically fragile structural formats, and shifts 
the model’s focus toward learning underlying chemical relationships rather than 
memorizing specific structural rules. By bypassing the reconstruction of full molecular 
graphs, fingerprint -based models inherently produce outputs that remain chemically 
interpretable and consistent with known structure–activity principles. This results in greater 
stability, higher reliability, and enhanced exploration of chemical space, making 
fingerprint-level generative modelling particularly advantageous for AMR drug discovery 
where model robustness and chemical validity are essential 
 
2.4.3 Novelty Measurement 
Assessing molecular novelty is a central challenge in AI-driven drug discovery, especially 
in fields such as antimicrobial research where the discovery of structurally unique 
compounds is essential for overcoming rapidly evolving resistance mechanisms. Novelty 
measurement aims to determine whether a generated compound or fingerprint represents a 
meaningful deviation from known chemical scaffolds rather than a minor variation of 
existing structures. However, accurately quantifying novelty is far from straightforward 
due to the complexity of molecular diversity and the limitations of commonly used 
similarity metrics. 
 
Most traditional novelty assessment approaches rely on single -metric evaluations such as 
Tanimoto similarity between binary fingerprints or simple distance -based measures 
between molecular embeddings. While useful, these metrics capture only surface -level 
similarity and often fail to reflect deeper structural or functional differences. For example, 
two molecules may share similar fingerprint overlaps due to common substructures, yet 
differ significantly in stereochemistry, topology, or functional group arrangement, features 
that greatly influence antimicrobial activity. In such cases, a similarity score alone may 
misrepresent true chemical novelty. Moreover, high-dimensional molecular spaces tend to 
exhibit sparse distributions where even small numerical differences may correspond to 
substantial structural variations, making threshold selection for novelty classification 
ambiguous and dataset-dependent. 15  
Another limitation in novelty assessment arises from the inherent biases within AMR 
datasets. Because active antibacterial compounds often share highly conserved scaffolds, 
novelty metrics may overestimate similarity simply due to the dominance of certain 
pharmacophores in the training data. Conversely, novelty metrics may underestimate 
similarity when generated compounds utilize alternative structural motifs that are 
underrepresented in the dataset. As a result, relying solely on fingerprint similarity may 
lead to misinterpretation of generative performance, either by incorrectly flagging 
innovative compounds as too similar or by accepting trivial modifications as novel. 
 
To overcome these issues, modern approaches integrate multiple complementary novelty 
assessment techniques. These may include combining cosine similarity, Tanimoto 
similarity, scaffold-based comparisons, substructure analysis, or dimensionality-reduction 
methods such as UMAP or t -SNE to visualize whether generated compounds occupy 
distinct regions of chemical space. UMAP, in particular, provides valuable qualitative 
insights by preserving both global and local structural relationships and illustrating whether 
generated fingerprints cluster separately from known actives. Such multi -dimensional 
evaluations support a more comprehensive understanding of chemical diversity and 
generative model performance. 
 
Fingerprint-level generative modelling, as used in this study, uniquely enhances novelty 
assessment by operating on structurally informative, high -dimensional binary 
representations that naturally emphasize substructure -level variations. Because the model 
does not attempt to reconstruct full molecular structures, variations in fingerprint space 
directly reflect learned chemical patterns rather than syntactic artefacts. This allows for 
clearer differentiation between new and existing chemical motifs and enables more reliable 
identification of structurally novel candidates with potential biological relevance. Overall, 
robust novelty measurement is critical for guiding antimicrobial discovery efforts toward 
unexplored chemical regions capable of addressing the global AMR crisis 
 
2.4.4 Reconstruction and Fingerprint Errors 
 
One of the major limitations of conventional molecular generative models, particularly 
SMILES-based VAEs, sequence -to-sequence models, and graph generative networks, is 
the difficulty of accurately reconstructing complete molecular structures. SMILES strings 
are highly sensitive to syntactic correctness, meaning that even a single misplaced character 
can result in an invalid or chemically impossible structure. Similarly, graph -based models 
often struggle with maintaining proper valence, aromaticity constraints, ring closures, and 
stereochemical configurations. These reconstruction challenges lead to high rates of invalid 
molecules, preventing the model from generating chemically meaningful outputs and 
severely restricting chemical space exploration. 
 
Reconstruction errors also distort the latent space learned by the model. When the decoder 
produces inaccurate or unstable molecular graphs, the latent representation fails to capture 
true structural relationships between compounds. As a result, the generative process 16  
becomes unreliable, with the decoder frequently producing unrealistic or redundant 
structures. Moreover, the reconstruction loss used during training amplifies these issues 
and forces the model to compromise diversity in order to reduce syntactic violations. This 
often results in mode collapse, where the model repetitively generates similar structures 
despite the potential diversity encoded in the latent space. 
 
Fingerprint-level modelling avoids these problems entirely. Instead of requiring the 
decoder to reconstruct a full chemical structure with strict syntactic and atomic constraints, 
the model directly operates on numerical fingerprint vectors. These binary vectors are 
inherently stable, free of syntactic rules, and straightforward to reconstruct because each 
bit simply represents the presence or absence of specific molecular substructures. By 
bypassing the reconstruction of full molecules, the framework eliminates the need to 
enforce valence checks, ring closures, or molecular grammar rules. This leads to more 
predictable training behaviour, reduced reconstruction error, and significantly improved 
chemical space navigation. Most importantly, fingerprint-level generation allows the model 
to focus on learning and generating chemically meaningful patterns rather than syntactic 
formats, making it especially suitable for AMR research where data scarcity and structural 
diversity are major challenges. 
2.4.5 Class Imbalance 
AMR datasets typically contain extremely few active molecules. A 386:1 inactive-to-active 
class ratio in the RecBCD dataset, resulted in: 
 
• Limited Structural diversity in known actives - Even among the few active 
compounds present, structural diversity is often narrow, as many antibacterial 
molecules originate from similar scaffolds or share common pharmacophores. This 
limited diversity makes it difficult for generative models to explore novel chemical 
space, as the training data does not represent the full range of possible antibacterial 
structural motifs. As a result, models tend to overfit to known patterns and struggle 
to generate truly innovative fingerprints or structures capable of overcoming 
resistance mechanisms. 
• Incomplete or Noisy annotations - AMR datasets frequently contain incomplete, 
ambiguous, or inconsistent activity labels. Variability in experimental conditions, 
assay protocols, bacterial strains, and measurement systems can lead to noisy labels 
or conflicting results. Such inconsistencies complicate model training and degrade 
predictive performance. Reliable annotations are crucial for guiding generative 
models and supervised classifiers, but inconsistencies can mislead the learning 
process and cause non-generalizable outcomes. 
• Lack of standardized AMR datasets - Unlike oncology or general pharmacology, 
where benchmark datasets are widely available, AMR research lacks standardized 
datasets that are sufficiently large, diverse, and uniformly annotated. This 
deficiency makes it difficult to compare methods across studies or benchmark 17  
generative and predictive models. Moreover, the absence of publicly available 
AMR-specific molecular libraries restricts collaborative progress and slows the 
development of AI-driven antibiotic discovery pipelines. 
• High cost and difficulty of generating new AMR data - Experimentally validating 
new antimicrobial compounds is resource -intensive. It requires specialized 
laboratory environments, microbial cultures, biosafety protocols, and advanced 
biochemical assays. These constraints limit the pace at which new labelled data can 
be generated, forcing researchers to depend heavily on computational approaches. 
However, without robust datasets, even advanced AI techniques may fail to achieve 
optimal performance. 
• Structural redundancy and chemical bias - AMR datasets often exhibit bias toward 
historically well -studied antibacterial classes , such as β -lactams, quinolones, 
tetracyclines, and aminoglycosides. This bias results in an overrepresentation of 
certain scaffolds and a lack of novel chemical shapes or topologies. Generative 
models trained on biased datasets may unintentionally reproduce structural 
redundancies rather than innovating beyond known antibacterial chemotypes. 
• Challenges in capturing complex biological resistance mechanisms – Resistance is 
a multifaceted phenomenon involving: 
 
• efflux pumps, 
• enzymatic degradation, 
• membrane modification, 
• target-site mutations, 
• biofilm formation, and 
• metabolic adaptation. 
 
Many AMR datasets capture only simplified activity outcomes without providing 
mechanistic or pathway -level context. This restricts AI models from learning deeper 
biological relationships that influence antibacterial effectiveness, ultimately limiting the 
generalizability of activity predictions or generative outputs. 
 
These limitations collectively demonstrate why traditional molecular generation techniques 
and predictive models often struggle when applied to AMR research. The small size, low 
diversity, imbalance, and noise levels in AMR datasets necessitate alternative approaches 
that are: 
 
• more data-efficient, 
• robust against imbalance, 
• less dependent on full molecule reconstruction, 18  
• capable of exploring chemical space with minimal structural bias. 
 
Fingerprint-level generative modelling, as used in this project, directly addresses many of 
these challenges by enabling meaningful exploration of chemical space even under severe 
data constraints. 
 
2.4.6 Generalization and Chemical Space Coverage 
Generalization and comprehensive chemical space coverage are critical requirements for 
any AI-driven molecular discovery system, particularly in antimicrobial research where 
identifying structurally novel compounds is essential to combating resistance. 
Generalization refers to the model’s ability to learn meaningful chemical features from the 
training data and apply this knowledge to generate or evaluate molecules that were not 
explicitly seen during training. However, achieving strong generalization in AMR-focused 
models is inherently challenging due to the limited diversity and biased nature of available 
datasets. When training data contains only a narrow selection of chemical scaffolds, often 
biased toward historically successful antibiotic classes, AI models may inadvertently learn 
overly specific patterns that restrict them to generating molecules within a confined, 
already-explored chemical region. This leads to a form of structural overfitting, where the 
model repeatedly outputs variations of known compounds instead of discovering new and 
potentially effective chemical frameworks. 
 
Chemical space coverage further complicates this issue. The total chemical universe is 
estimated to contain between 10³⁰ to 10⁶⁰ drug -like molecules, far exceeding what any 
experimental or computational dataset can capture. A reliable generative model should be 
capable of navigating this vast chemical landscape and exploring regions far beyond those 
defined by the training data. Yet many molecular generative models, particularly those 
using SMILES or graph reconstruction, tend to produce molecules that are clustered tightly 
around the training distribution, demonstrating limited capacity for extrapolation. This 
restricted exploration is often caused by stringent reconstruction constraints, high 
dependency on syntactic rules, or latent spaces that fail to accurately encode global 
chemical relationships. 
 
Fingerprint-based generative models, however, offer a more flexible and robust alternative 
for chemical space exploration. Unlike SMILES-based systems, fingerprint representations 
abstract chemical structures into high -dimensional vectors that naturally capture 
substructure-level variations and enable smoother latent transitions. When embedded into 
a VAE framework, these fingerprints allow the model to traverse latent space more freely, 
discovering combinations of chemical features that may not directly appear in the training 
set but still remain chemically meaningful. This increases the likelihood of generating 
compounds that lie in unexplored chemical regions while maintaining relevance to known 
antibacterial patterns. Moreover, because fingerprint-based VAEs do not rely on syntactic 
molecular reconstruction, they avoid common pitfalls such as invalid molecules, structural 
duplication, or decoder collapse, thereby enhancing generalization and diversity. 19  
Ensuring broad chemical space coverage also requires evaluating whether generated 
molecules occupy distinct regions compared to known actives. Techniques such as UMAP 
and t-SNE provide valuable insights by projecting high-dimensional fingerprints into two- 
dimensional visual maps, making it possible to observe clustering behaviour, separation 
patterns, and latent manifold structures. Clear separation between generated and known 
compounds indicates that the generative model has successfully explored previously 
uncharted chemical territories. Conversely, overlapping clusters may suggest inadequate 
generalization or excessive structural bias. 
 
Ultimately, generalization and chemical space coverage determine the real-world utility of 
AI models in antibiotic discovery. Models that produce only minor variations of known 
molecules contribute little toward addressing AMR, whereas systems capable of traversing 
and expanding into novel chemical regions hold the potential to uncover entirely new 
classes of antimicrobial agents. Therefore, strengthening generalization and ensuring 
broad, diverse chemical space coverage remain essential goals for advancing AI -driven 
drug discovery pipelines. 
 
2.4.7 Continuous Improvement and Updates 
Continuous improvement and regular updates are essential for maintaining the 
effectiveness of AI models used in antimicrobial drug discovery, particularly because AMR 
is a rapidly evolving biological challenge. Bacterial populations adapt quickly through 
mutation, gene acquisition, and metabolic reprogramming, meaning that models trained on 
older datasets may gradually lose relevance as new resistance patterns emerge. Without 
periodic updates, predictive accuracy may decline and generative models may begin 
producing compounds that no longer align with current biological mechanisms of 
resistance. 
 
To remain effective, AI frameworks must integrate new experimental results, updated 
activity labels, refined molecular descriptors, and expanded training datasets as they 
become available. Improvements in laboratory assays, cheminformatics tools, or new 
computational techniques, such as enhanced fingerprint representations or updated VAE 
architectures, should also be incorporated to strengthen model performance. Regular 
retraining helps correct earlier biases, improve generalization, and enhance the model’s 
ability to identify rare but meaningful antimicrobial patterns. 
 
Additionally, continuous feedback from downstream stages, such as in -silico scoring or 
wet-lab validation, provides valuable insight for iterative refinement. By establishing a 
feedback-driven loop, the system can evolve in parallel with scientific advances and 
bacterial resistance trends. Thus, continuous improvement is not merely a technical 
enhancement but a necessary component to ensure that AI -driven drug discovery remains 
adaptive, robust, and aligned with the dynamic nature of AMR research. 20
